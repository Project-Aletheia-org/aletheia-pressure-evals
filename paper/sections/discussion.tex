\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What this does and does not support}

The central finding is about measurement, not about model behavior. On the same
180 blinded outputs, with an identical rubric, blinding, and prompt, the answer
to a preregistered behavioral question inverts depending on which judge scores
the text: a small open-weight judge reports no effect of an autonomy-preserving
instruction, and a frontier judge reports a moderate, multiply-corrected effect
in the same data. The two judges agree at chance on every rubric dimension. We
therefore treat ``does goal pressure make these models more manipulative, and
does an autonomy instruction help?'' as a question whose \emph{measured} answer is
evaluator-dependent in this regime, and we report that dependence as the result.

This does \emph{not} support a claim that either judge is correct. We have no
human-labeled ground truth, so we cannot say the frontier judge is detecting real
manipulation the open-weight judge misses, or that the open-weight judge is
correctly seeing benign text the frontier judge over-reads. Both single-judge
conclusions --- and the significant two-judge consensus effect --- are consistent
with the data; nothing in this study adjudicates between them. Nor does the study
support any claim about model intent, internal states, or evaluation awareness: it
measures generated text under a controlled prompt only.

\subsection{No ground-truth adjudicator}

The single most important caveat is the absence of a reference standard. A blinded
human-annotation arm was designed (Section~\ref{sec:judges}) but \emph{no human
ratings have been collected}; it is therefore not part of the evidentiary basis
of this paper and validates nothing here. Because the deterministic audit is a
coarse keyword detector that agrees with \emph{both} judges that overt lexical
manipulation is rare, it cannot break the tie on the subtler, contextual coding
where the judges actually diverge. Until human (or otherwise independent) labels
exist, evaluator disagreement is the finding, not a step toward resolving which
judge to believe.

\subsection{A possible same-family / self-evaluation effect}

The frontier judge's effect is concentrated in the outputs of \texttt{qwen3:4b},
which is also the open-weight judge, and the open-weight judge floors its own
outputs most completely of any subject model. This is consistent with the
self-preference and self-evaluation biases documented for larger judges
\citep{wataoka2024selfpreference,pombal2026rubricspb}, now appearing as a
measurement-\emph{stability} problem rather than a preference inflation. We
deliberately stop short of a causal claim. We cannot separate a genuine
same-family blind spot from a simpler explanation in which a small judge with a
compressed dynamic range simply fails to resolve within-condition variation and
does so most severely on the text closest to its own distribution. Distinguishing
these requires manipulating judge/subject family relatedness directly, which this
design does not do. What we can say is narrow and, we think, useful: a
configuration that many practitioners would accept as an independent multi-judge
validation --- three open-weight judges cross-checking each other --- reproduced
the same blind spot rather than catching it, because all three share the
floor/ceiling behavior on the discriminating dimensions.

\subsection{We cannot infer evaluator motive or causal bias}

``Evaluator-sensitive'' here is an observational statement about scores, not an
attribution of bias to any model. We do not claim the open-weight judge is
``biased'' in favor of its family, nor that the frontier judge is unbiased; either
could be closer to a future human standard. The result stands whichever way that
adjudication eventually falls, because the claim is only that the behavioral
conclusion is unstable across the evaluators actually used.

\subsection{Implications for LLM-judged evaluation}

If a rubric-based, blinded, multi-judge evaluation --- a fairly careful setup ---
can inject a conclusion-flipping dependence on judge choice, then single-judge
behavioral conclusions about small open-weight models should be reported with the
judge as an explicit part of the claim, and multi-judge panels should be checked
for shared failure modes (here, a common floor on the discriminating dimensions)
rather than assumed independent. Judge agreement was markedly higher on
dimensions near the floor/ceiling than on those requiring finer discrimination,
so aggregate agreement statistics computed over easy dimensions can mask
instability exactly where the substantive signal lives.
