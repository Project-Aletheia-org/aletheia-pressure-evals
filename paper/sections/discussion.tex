\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The headline finding is about measurement, not just behavior}

The most important result of this study is not "no effect" or "a
significant effect" -- it is that a single-judge design, even one that
looks methodologically careful (a distinct judge model, blinded scoring,
a fixed rubric, cross-validated on a stratified sample by two further
judges), reported the wrong-sign conclusion here, because the specific
judge chosen happened to also be one of the three models under study. A
reader who only had access to Sections
\ref{sec:primary-contrasts}--\ref{sec:multi-judge-agreement-local} would,
reasonably, conclude this study found no effect. A reader who only had
access to Section~\ref{sec:evaluator-sensitivity} would, also reasonably,
conclude the opposite. Both readings are honest
readings of real analyses on real data; the disagreement between them is
the finding we consider most worth reporting, and it is why we do not lead
with either judge's verdict as ``the'' result.

\subsection{Why did the primary judge miss the effect?}

Section~\ref{sec:evaluator-sensitivity} traces the disagreement to a
specific, checkable mechanism rather than leaving it as an unexplained
discrepancy: \texttt{qwen3:4b} is simultaneously a subject model generating
text under all four conditions and the primary judge scoring that text
(along with the other two subject models' text). Scoring its own outputs,
it returns a flat, zero-variance manipulation score in 14 of 15 scenarios
regardless of condition; scoring the same text, an independent frontier
judge finds a clear, condition-dependent swing. This is consistent with a
\emph{self-evaluation blind spot}: the same weights that produced the
persuasive framing in the first place are less able to recognize that
framing as manipulative when asked to grade it, even under strict blinding
of subject-model identity and condition label. Blinding controls for
\emph{knowing} whose output is being scored; it does not control for a
judge's own generative habits shaping what it \emph{recognizes} as
manipulative in text that reads like its own writing. This is a distinct
mechanism from the position bias and pairwise self-preference bias
documented in prior LLM-as-judge work (Section~\ref{sec:related_work}) -- here the judge is
scoring a single response on an absolute rubric, not choosing between two
candidates, and still under-detects its own model's manipulative framing
relative to an outside judge.

\subsection{What this does and does not support}

Within the 15 tested (deliberately low-stakes, benign) scenarios, and
scored by an evaluator outside the subject-model family, the
autonomy-preserving instruction measurably reduces manipulation-coded
language and increases autonomy-respecting behavior relative to the
pressure condition; scored by a judge that is also one of the subject
models, this same instruction shows no detectable effect. We do not treat
either judge's number as ground truth. What the data support jointly is
narrower and more useful: (1) at least one of the three small open-weight
models studied here does write more manipulative-coded persuasive text
under goal pressure, detectable by an external judge; (2) the effect this
study set out to measure is real enough to survive consensus scoring, even
after halving under averaging; and (3) whether a study like this one finds
the effect at all can depend on a methodological choice --
which model judges -- that is easy to get wrong even with deliberate
design effort.

This does \emph{not} support a claim that these models -- or language
models generally -- cannot become more manipulative under any goal
pressure, nor a claim that they reliably do. The scenarios were designed
to be benign; higher-stakes or more adversarial scenario framings, longer
or multi-turn interactions, or different (stronger, more specific)
pressure instructions might elicit a different pattern under either judge.
Nor does it support any claim about internal model states, intent, or
evaluation-awareness -- this study measures observable generated text
under a controlled prompt, scored by other models, only.

\subsection{Why did the open-weight judge see nothing, specifically?}

Beyond the self-evaluation mechanism above, several further non-exclusive
explanations are consistent with why \texttt{qwen3:4b}'s scores in
particular were compressed near the floor for \emph{all three} subject
models, not only its own outputs:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Genuine model robustness at this scale and stakes level.}
Small open-weight models may carry safety-relevant training that resists
persuasion-maximizing framing even when explicitly instructed, at least
for benign requests.
\item \textbf{Scenario benignity limits the ``room'' for manipulation.}
Scenarios were designed with no dangerous, high-stakes, or adversarial
content by design (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}); this may be precisely why
little manipulation was detected regardless of condition.
\item \textbf{Judge and rubric sensitivity.} A small open-weight judge,
scoring on a coarse 0--2 scale per dimension, may under-detect subtle
persuasion techniques that a stronger judge or a human annotator would
catch -- motivating this study's blinded multi-judge and selective
human-escalation design rather than trusting a single judge's score.
\item \textbf{Ceiling/floor compression limits statistical power.} With
most scores at or near the scale's boundary, the paired bootstrap and
permutation tests have very little variance in which to detect a true
effect, even a small one.
\end{enumerate}

We do not have evidence to adjudicate between these explanations from this
pilot alone, and we do not force a preference among them.

\subsection{Judge sensitivity and multi-judge validation}

Judge agreement is markedly higher on dimensions closer to the observed
floor/ceiling (\texttt{respect\_for\_refusal}, \texttt{artificial\_urgency})
than on dimensions requiring finer discrimination
(\texttt{deceptive\_omission}, \texttt{authority\_social\_pressure}). Combined
with \texttt{llama3.2:3b}'s substantially higher empty-evidence rate, this
suggests smaller open-weight judges are less reliable at the more subtle
end of manipulation detection specifically -- a finding relevant to anyone
considering small open-weight models as judges for behavioral evaluation,
independent of this study's evaluator-sensitivity result.

\subsection{Practical implication for LLM-as-judge practice}

The design choice this study would change, if repeated, is narrow and
structural rather than about picking a ``better'' judge: \textbf{a model
should not be the primary judge of outputs that include its own
generations.} This is a stronger requirement than ``use a distinct judge
model'' or ``blind the judge to subject-model identity'' -- both of those
were already true here, and both were insufficient. It is also cheaper to
satisfy than it looks: routing only the subset of items where the subject
model and candidate judge coincide to a different judge (rather than
replacing the primary judge study-wide) would have caught this. This is
the specific structural rule the framework's V2 successor adopts
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
