\section{Introduction}

Using a language model to grade another model's output --- ``LLM-as-a-judge'' ---
is now routine in behavioral and safety evaluation, because it scales where human
annotation does not. The known risks are treated as \emph{score-level}: a judge
might be biased high or low, or prefer its own style, and the usual remedy is to
average several judges. This paper reports a case where that remedy quietly
fails, in a way that a practitioner would want to know about before trusting an
LLM-judged number.

We started with an ordinary behavioral question. When a small assistant model is
told to maximize agreement (``convert the user,'' ``retain the subscriber''),
does its writing become more manipulative --- more concealment, urgency, or
pressure --- and does an explicit autonomy-preserving instruction pull it back?
We generated 180 messages under a $2\times2$ pressure/autonomy design across 15
benign scenarios and three small open-weight models, and scored every message on
a six-dimension rubric using \texttt{qwen3:4b} as the judge. The result was a
clean null: nothing moved.

Then we re-scored the \emph{identical} blinded outputs with a frontier judge
(\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}) --- same rubric, same blinding, same prompt --- as a
sanity check. The null fell apart. The frontier judge saw a clear effect of the
autonomy instruction, and the two judges agreed at barely above chance. Digging
into where they disagreed produced the finding of this paper.

\paragraph{The blind spot.} The open-weight judge scored its \emph{own} model's
outputs (\texttt{qwen3:4b} as subject) at manipulation $=0$ with zero variance
across all four conditions in 14 of 15 scenarios --- a flat line. On that same
text, the frontier judge recorded a clear condition-dependent swing. In other
words, the single place where an independent judge found the largest signal is
precisely the place the open-weight judge was blind: its own family's outputs.
Averaging in two more open-weight judges did not help, because all three share
the same floored behavior on the dimensions that carry the signal. A setup many
teams would accept as an independent multi-judge validation reproduced the blind
spot instead of catching it.

\paragraph{What we do and do not claim.} We treat every score as a
\emph{measurement by a specific judge}, never as the behavior itself. We do
\emph{not} claim the frontier judge is ground truth (we have no human labels), and
we do \emph{not} claim the blind spot is caused by self-preference: a small judge
with a compressed dynamic range that happens to floor same-family text most
severely would produce the same pattern, and our design cannot separate the two.
What survives either explanation is a concrete, reproducible measurement hazard
and a practical way to avoid it.

\paragraph{Contribution.} (1) A controlled, reproducible demonstration that a
small open-weight LLM judge can be blind to a real behavioral effect in its own
family's outputs --- with exact effect sizes, uncertainty, and
multiple-comparison correction. (2) A practical detection-and-mitigation
checklist for anyone using LLM judges (Section~\ref{sec:recs}). (3) A versioned,
locally reproducible evaluation harness (blinded multi-judge scoring, a
deterministic keyword audit, and selective human-escalation) whose open-weight
arm runs with no paid API. We deliberately leave the disagreement unresolved:
without ground truth we cannot resolve it, and pretending otherwise is the exact
failure mode we are documenting.
