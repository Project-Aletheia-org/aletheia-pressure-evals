\begin{abstract}
When an AI assistant is optimized to achieve a persuasive objective, does
its visible communication become more manipulative, and can an explicit
autonomy-preserving instruction reduce that effect? We study this question
as a controlled behavioral experiment: three small open-weight language
models (\texttt{qwen3:4b}, \texttt{gemma3:4b}, \texttt{llama3.2:3b}) each
generate a message under one of four system-prompt conditions
(\emph{baseline}, \emph{pressure}, \emph{autonomy},
\emph{pressure\_autonomy}) across 15 benign persuasion scenarios (180
outputs total), scored on a six-dimension manipulation/autonomy rubric by
a blinded open-weight judge and cross-validated against two additional
independent judges on a stratified 60-item sample. Contrary to our
confirmatory hypotheses, under the open-weight primary judge we find
\textbf{no detectable increase in manipulation-coded language under the
pressure condition, and no detectable change from adding the autonomy
instruction}: manipulation scores are compressed near the floor (mean
0.04--0.11 of a possible 0--8 in every condition) and autonomy scores near
the ceiling (mean 3.96--4.00 of 0--4), with all three primary
paired-contrast permutation tests yielding $p \geq 0.63$. A subsequent
extension (reported in the repository, and being integrated into a
forthcoming revision of this paper) re-scores the identical blinded items
with an independent frontier judge and finds this null does \emph{not}
hold, indicating the result is evaluator-sensitive; that analysis is
summarized in \texttt{reports/judge\_sensitivity\_summary.md}. We report
the original null honestly as a preliminary, model-, scenario-scale-, and
\emph{evaluator}-dependent finding, and contribute a versioned, locally
reproducible evaluation framework -- blinded multi-judge scoring, a
deterministic diagnostic audit, and a selective human-escalation
methodology that scales without requiring exhaustive manual annotation.
\end{abstract}
