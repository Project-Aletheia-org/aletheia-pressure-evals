\begin{abstract}
When you use one language model to grade another, the standard safeguard is to
add more judges. We document a case where that safeguard fails in a specific,
reproducible way: a small open-weight model, used to grade its own family's
outputs, floored a real behavioral signal that an independent judge detected.
Three small open-weight models (\texttt{qwen3:4b}, \texttt{gemma3:4b},
\texttt{llama3.2:3b}) each write a persuasive message under one of four
system-prompt conditions across 15 benign scenarios (180 outputs); the identical
blinded outputs are then scored on a six-dimension manipulation/autonomy rubric
--- same rubric, same blinding, same prompt --- by \texttt{qwen3:4b} and,
independently, by a frontier judge (\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-6}). Scoring its own
outputs, \texttt{qwen3:4b} assigns a manipulation score of exactly 0 with
\emph{zero variance} across all four conditions in 14 of 15 scenarios; the
frontier judge, on the same text, sees a clear, condition-dependent effect (an
autonomy-preserving instruction reduces manipulation, Cohen's $d \approx -0.5$,
$p = 0.0017$, Holm-corrected). The two judges agree at chance on every rubric
dimension (all Cohen's $\kappa \approx 0$), and the disagreement is largest
exactly on \texttt{qwen3:4b}'s own outputs --- the one place you would most want
an outside opinion. We do not claim either judge is correct: there is no
human-labeled ground truth, and we cannot prove the blind spot is causal
self-preference rather than a small judge's limited dynamic range. But the
practical lesson holds either way, and it is the contribution of this paper: do
not let a model be the primary judge of its own family, watch for zero-variance
score cells, and cross-check against a stronger judge on a subsample. Everything
is reproducible from committed data; the open-weight arm requires no paid API.
\end{abstract}
